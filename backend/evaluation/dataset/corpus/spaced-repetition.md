# Spaced repetition in a study deck

## Retention and the forgetting curve

Imagine studying a diagram until you can draw it from memory. An immediate redraw shows that you can still retrieve the diagram after a few seconds. It tells you much less about an attempt next week. A forgetting curve plots the probability of a successful recall against the delay since practice. Recall often falls quickly at first and then more slowly. Its shape depends on what was learned, prior knowledge and the cue used on the test. There is no universal half-life for a page of notes.

To measure retention in a deck, count correct first attempts at a given delay. Keep attempts with hints in a separate column. Otherwise a large pile of newly learned cards can make the deck look healthier just because their last exposure was recent. Compare cards reviewed after similar delays and record how many attempts went into each estimate.

## Active recall

Cover the answer, read a cue, and produce a response before checking the original. This order matters. A definition can look familiar while it is visible even if its first word will not come to mind without help. Active recall makes the missing step observable. Reading the correction immediately after an honest attempt gives the learner a precise error to repair. Merely turning the card over and nodding produces a different kind of evidence.

A useful cue asks for one decision that can be marked. For a diagram, hide the labels and ask which arrow carries a particular signal. If two arrows are plausible, revise the cue before assigning a low score. At the next sitting, draw the arrow from memory before comparing it with the diagram. A second look at the printed arrow measures recognition again.

## Mapping four buttons to SM-2

The original SM-2 procedure accepts a quality grade from zero to five. A four-button deck needs an explicit mapping before its intervals can be interpreted. In the worked deck below, Again records grade two, Hard grade three, Good grade four and Easy grade five. The grade represents the response that actually occurred. An explanation read from the back of the card cannot turn a missed first attempt into a success.

The deck keeps a count of successive successful repetitions, an interval in days and an ease factor for each card. A short correction inside a session is recorded separately from the due date for the next scheduled repetition. Two cards seen on the same day can consequently have very different next due dates.

### Again

Choose Again after a lapse, when the target could not be produced unaided. A lapse resets the successful repetition count to zero. Read the correction, hide it, and retry in the same session after some other cards. That retry checks that the correction was understood. The long interval earned before the lapse no longer determines the next scheduled step.

For example, a learner might retrieve the name of a process but reverse its input and output. If the direction was the point of the cue, that is a miss. Write down the direction, cover it and reconstruct the pair later in the sitting. Counting the exposed correction as a scheduled success would hide the uncertainty that caused the lapse.

### Hard

Hard records a correct answer retrieved with serious difficulty. In this deck it counts as a successful repetition, even if the learner needed time to reconstruct a missing link. A hint revealing that link would change the evidence. Keep notes about hints next to the attempt so a hesitant success stays distinguishable from a miss followed by reading.

The lower passing grade reduces the ease factor used for later growth. If a long definition is consistently hard because it contains several unrelated claims, split the cue and observe each claim separately. Shortening every interval for the whole deck would also penalize material the learner already produces reliably.

### Good

Choose Good for an accurate, unaided recall that took ordinary effort. The first successful repetition sets the interval to one day. A second consecutive success sets it to six days. After the starting steps the current interval grows using the ease factor. In the four-button mapping the grade for Good leaves that factor unchanged.

Consider a learner who draws the right diagram and can explain the selected arrow without peeking. Mark the response before reading the explanation on the reverse. If this was the first success, schedule the next visit for tomorrow even though the diagram feels clear now. The next result supplies evidence at a longer delay.

### Easy

Easy describes a prompt and complete retrieval, including any distinction the cue asked for. It raises the ease factor in this mapping. A card can feel easy because its wording gives the answer away. Test that possibility by changing the cue on a later occasion, for example by asking for the cause of an observation instead of its name.

The first two scheduled successes still use the starting intervals in this worked deck. Later reviews show the effect of a larger multiplier. Two past successes do not guarantee a third one after a long break, so the learner still attempts every due card before uncovering it.

## Interval growth and the ease floor

SM-2 starts the ease factor at 2.5 and uses a lower bound of 1.3. Higher grades can continue to raise the factor without a fixed upper bound. Once the starting repetitions are complete, multiply the previous interval by the ease factor to get the next interval, rounding up to whole days. For instance, an interval of six days and a factor of 2.5 give a nominal gap of fifteen days. The next attempt measures how well that schedule worked for this card.

Keep a separate record if a calendar rule brings a card forward for a holiday or an exam. That change in the actual delay is different from a change in the ease factor. Without both values the reviewer cannot tell whether an apparent improvement came from stronger retention or from testing the same material sooner.

## Exam readiness and interleaving

A readiness check uses delayed, unaided attempts on a mixture of topics. Interleaving asks the learner to choose a method as well as execute it. A block of nearly identical exercises reveals the next method in advance. In a mixed set, selecting the wrong method is a visible error even when the subsequent arithmetic is flawless.

Reserve some unfamiliar problems for a timed practice sitting. Mix topics without printing their chapter labels and mark the first complete response to each one. Compare these results with the deck's delayed recall results. A learner who recalls a rule but chooses it in the wrong setting needs practice distinguishing cases. Scheduling another copy of the definition does little to exercise that choice.
